INTERLOCK OR TRANSFER SWITCH.

How Biloxi and Gulfport homeowners hook up a portable or standby generator safely for hurricane outages — without backfeeding power lines or overspending on extra subpanels.

Every hurricane season along the Mississippi Gulf Coast brings the same conversation: power goes out across Biloxi, Gulfport, or Ocean Springs for three days, and homeowners either run extension cords through cracked doorways or wonder how to power the whole panel cleanly from a portable generator.

You have two proper electrical methods for tying a portable generator into your home: a main-panel interlock kit or a manual transfer switch. Both isolate your house from the utility grid cleanly. Choosing the right one depends on your panel brand, breaker spaces, and how flexible you want to be during a multi-day outage.

First rule: never backfeed a dryer outlet

Using a double-male cord plugged into a 240V dryer or welder receptacle is unsafe and strictly unacceptable electrical practice. Without a physical mechanical interlock separating utility power from generator power, electricity flows backward onto street utility transformers — stepping 240 volts right back up to medium-voltage line levels where linemen work during storm restoration. It also risks an arc fault and fire the instant utility power returns while the main breaker was left closed.

Option 1: Main-panel interlock kit

An interlock kit is a machined metal plate installed on your main breaker panel cover paired with a dedicated 2-pole backfed generator breaker at the top of the bus bar and an exterior power inlet box. The sliding metal bracket mechanically blocks the generator breaker from turning ON unless your MAIN utility breaker is physically flipped OFF — and vice versa.

  • Pros: Access to every breaker already in your home. Need the upstairs fan instead of the guest bedroom circuit? Flip one breaker off and another on. Usually less wall clutter and more cost-effective when your main panel supports a manufacturer/listed interlock bracket.
  • Cons: Requires an available top 2-pole slot right below or opposite the main breaker and a compatible listed bracket for your panel brand and vintage. Also requires turning heavy loads (electric oven, dryer, water heater) off yourself before energizing the inlet.

Option 2: Manual transfer switch

A manual transfer switch sits next to your main panel as a small dedicated subpanel wired directly to 6, 8, or 10 pre-selected circuits (such as your fridge, kitchen plugs, bedroom outlets, lighting, and well pump). Each circuit has its own LINE / OFF / GEN toggle.

  • Pros: Works even when an older panel lacks compatible interlock hardware or top breaker spaces, or when you have an outdoor main disconnect separate from an indoor branch panel. Simple labeled switches mean anyone in the household can toggle utility vs generator safely.
  • Cons: Only the pre-wired circuits receive generator power, and installation costs slightly more in hardware and wiring time compared to a single panel interlock.

30-amp vs. 50-amp exterior inlet

Pairing the right weatherproof inlet box with your generator cord avoids tripping the generator breaker on startup:

  • 30A (NEMA L14-30, up to 7,200W continuous): Plenty for refrigerators, chest freezers, Wi-Fi, TVs, lights, microwave, and a window AC unit or 120V portable unit.
  • 50A (CS6375 / NEMA 14-50, up to 12,000W): Built for large dual-fuel portables, well pumps, and homes adding an HVAC soft-start kit to pull central AC cleanly during humid August outages.
